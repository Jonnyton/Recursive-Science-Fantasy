# Fantasy Writing System v0.1

A living document of craft principles for writing compelling fantasy fiction at the chapter level. This document is the experimental artifact — it will be iteratively improved by an AI research agent.

---

## 1. Chapter Architecture

A chapter is not a long scene — it's a unit of story with its own dramatic shape. It opens with a hook that earns the reader's time, develops through multiple beats that complicate the situation, and closes on a turning point that changes something and compels the reader forward. Plan the chapter's emotional trajectory: where does the character start, and where must they end up?

**Rule:** Every chapter should leave the character (or the reader's understanding) in a different place than where it started. If nothing has changed, the chapter hasn't earned its length.

## 2. Scene Transitions

The space between scenes is where amateurs lose readers. A transition must do three things: reorient in time and place, shift emotional register, and maintain narrative momentum. The best transitions create a subtle connection between the ending of one scene and the opening of the next — an echo of image, emotion, or theme that makes the cut feel intentional rather than arbitrary.

**Rule:** Never begin a new scene by re-establishing information the reader already has. Start at the moment something new happens.

## 3. Pacing Across Length

A full chapter runs approximately 4000 words and contains 3-5 distinct scenes or beats, separated by scene breaks ("* * *" on their own line). Each scene should run 600-1200 words, with its own mini-arc: a small goal, an obstacle, and a shift. This multi-scene structure creates natural rhythm.

A chapter must breathe. Relentless action exhausts; unbroken reflection bores. Alternate between compression (fast, tense, external) and expansion (slow, reflective, internal). Use short paragraphs and punchy sentences to accelerate. Use longer, more rhythmic prose to decelerate. The reader should feel the tempo shifting like music.

Structure the pacing in waves: Scene 1 (medium tension — establish situation), Scene 2 (rising — complication), Scene 3 (peak — confrontation or revelation), Scene 4 (falling — aftermath and processing), Scene 5 (pivot — new question or decision that ends the chapter). Not every chapter needs all five, but aim for at least three distinct scenes with clear breaks between them.

**Rule:** After every high-intensity beat, give the reader (and the character) a moment to absorb what happened — but make that quiet moment do double duty by advancing character or worldbuilding.

## 4. Worldbuilding Through Immersion

Ground your fantasy world in concrete, specific details woven into the action. Instead of telling the reader about a magic system, show a character using it and paying its cost. Every fantastical element should have texture — what does magic smell like? What does a ward feel like when you pass through it? Introduce worldbuilding through character interaction, never through narrator lectures.

**Rule:** The reader should learn how the world works by watching characters navigate it, not by being told.

## 5. Character Voice and Interiority

Each point-of-view character should have a distinct internal vocabulary shaped by their background. A blacksmith thinks in metaphors of heat and metal. A thief notices exits and valuables. This isn't just dialogue — it's narration filtered through a specific mind. Over the course of a chapter, the character's interiority should deepen: we should know them better by the end than the beginning.

**Rule:** Before writing a chapter, ask: "What would this character notice first in every setting, and what word would they use for it?"

## 6. Prose Craft

Favor concrete nouns and active verbs over adjectives and adverbs. Read your sentences aloud — if you stumble, rewrite. Avoid filter words ("she saw," "he felt," "they noticed") — put the reader directly in the experience. Vary sentence structure to match emotional content: short punches for action, longer flowing rhythms for reflection, fragments for shock.

**Rule:** After drafting, do one pass where you cut every word that doesn't earn its place.

## 7. Magic and Wonder

The best fantasy makes the reader feel genuine wonder. This comes not from power levels or systematic rules, but from *cost, mystery, and beauty*. Magic should be unsettling, awe-inspiring, or bittersweet — never routine. Even in a world where magic is common, the prose should treat it as remarkable.

**Rule:** Every use of magic should cost something — energy, sanity, memory, years of life, or moral compromise.

## 8. Emotional Truth

Fantasy settings are imaginary, but emotions must be real. Anchor fantastical experiences in universal feelings. The reader may never fight a dragon, but they know what it feels like to face something that could destroy them. A chapter's emotional arc matters as much as its plot arc.

**Rule:** For every fantastical plot beat, identify the real human emotion underneath it and write toward that.

## 9. Chapter Openings and Closings

Open in motion — the reader should feel they've arrived in the middle of something. Avoid the "waking up" opening and the "settling in" opening. Close on a pivot, not a resolution: a revelation, a decision, a question that won't let the reader stop. The last line of a chapter is the most important sentence you'll write.

**Rule:** Your opening paragraph should contain a concrete action, a sensory detail, and an unanswered question. Your closing paragraph should change what the reader thinks they know.

## 10. Originality

Fantasy has deep genre conventions. Use them as a foundation, then subvert, complicate, or recontextualize them. Avoid the first image that comes to mind — it's probably a cliché. Find the second or third. Surprising metaphors, unexpected character reactions, and unconventional narrative choices are what separate memorable fiction from competent fiction.

**Rule:** For every scene, ask: "What would the reader expect to happen here?" Then consider doing something different — not for shock value, but because reality is stranger and more interesting than convention.
