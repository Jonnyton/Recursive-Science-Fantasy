# Fantasy Writing System v2.0

A living document of craft principles for writing compelling fantasy fiction at the chapter level. Organized by priority: emotional truth and character drive everything; technique serves them.

---

## 1. The Character's Wound

Every POV character carries a foundational lie or wound — a belief about themselves or the world that is wrong but feels true. This wound drives their decisions, shapes their perceptions, and creates the emotional engine of the chapter. Before writing, identify: what does this character believe that will be tested by this chapter's events? How does this belief distort what they notice, how they interpret others' actions, and what they choose to do?

The wound should never be stated directly. It surfaces through pattern: the character misreads kindness as manipulation (because they were betrayed), avoids commitment (because they lost someone), or takes reckless risks (because they believe they deserve to be hurt). The reader should feel the wound before they can name it.

**Rule:** Every chapter-level decision the character makes should be traceable to their wound. If a choice doesn't connect to who they are at their most broken, the chapter lacks emotional architecture.

## 2. Dialogue as Character Revelation

Dialogue is not a delivery mechanism for plot information. Every line of speech should reveal something about the speaker — their education, their fears, their relationship to power, their sense of humor, their blind spots. Characters never say exactly what they mean. They deflect, they perform, they test each other.

Each speaker should sound unmistakably themselves. A soldier clips words and states facts. A scholar qualifies and hedges. A merchant frames everything in terms of value and exchange. A child interrupts and circles back. Differentiate speakers through vocabulary, sentence length, what they choose to address and what they ignore.

The best dialogue has subtext — the conversation underneath the conversation. Two characters arguing about whether to take the mountain pass are also arguing about who gets to lead. A healer explaining treatment is also establishing dominance over a patient who won't rest.

Never let dialogue run longer than three exchanges without an action beat — a gesture, a glance, a physical task that continues while characters talk. These beats do triple duty: they break up walls of speech, ground the reader in the physical scene, and reveal what characters do with their bodies when their mouths are saying something else. A character who agrees while backing toward the door is telling two stories at once.

**Rule:** If you can swap two characters' dialogue and nobody would notice, the dialogue has failed. If removing a line of dialogue loses no character information, cut it.

## 3. Emotional Truth Over Fantasy Convention

Fantasy settings are imaginary but emotions must be real. Anchor every fantastical experience in universal human feeling. The reader may never hear a dying god's thoughts, but they know what it feels like to hear someone they love asking for help they can't give.

Never name an emotion directly. "She felt grief" is a failure. Instead: her hands wouldn't stop folding the cloth, smoothing a crease, folding again, because if she stopped her hands would shake and if they shook she'd know why. The reader should feel the emotion through the body, through action, through the suddenly wrong detail — the meal that's still warm, the chair still pushed back.

Earned emotion comes from accumulation, not announcement. Build through small, specific moments so the big beats land. A character's breaking point hits harder when we've watched them hold it together through three scenes of small indignities.

**Rule:** For every fantastical plot beat, identify the real human emotion underneath it and write toward that. The magic is the vehicle; the feeling is the destination.

## 4. Chapter Architecture

A chapter is a unit of story with its own dramatic shape. It opens with a hook that earns the reader's time, develops through 3-5 beats that complicate the situation, and closes on a turning point that changes something. Plan the emotional trajectory: where does the character start emotionally, and where must they arrive?

Structure in waves: establish (medium tension), complicate (rising), confront (peak), process (falling), pivot (new question that ends the chapter). Not every chapter needs all five beats, but aim for at least three distinct movements with clear scene breaks ("* * *") between them.

**Rule:** Every chapter should leave the character in a different emotional place than where they started. If nothing has changed internally, the chapter hasn't earned its length.

## 5. Character Voice as Prose Engine

Each POV character reshapes the prose itself. A blacksmith's narration follows process: first this, then this, and if this goes wrong, that. A thief's narration scans constantly: exits, angles, what's in pockets. A healer reads bodies involuntarily: the way someone favors a leg, the color under their nails.

This isn't decoration — it's the fundamental texture of the chapter. Sentence structure, paragraph rhythm, what gets noticed and what gets skipped, the metaphors that come naturally — all sourced from the character's specific domain of experience. A cartographer sees arguments as boundary disputes. A smith reads mood in the color of heated metal.

**Rule:** Before writing, ask: "What would this character notice first in every setting? What would they never notice? What word would they use that nobody else would?"

## 6. Prose Craft

Favor concrete nouns and active verbs. Avoid filter words ("she saw," "he felt," "they noticed") — put the reader directly in the experience. Vary sentence structure to match emotional content: short punches for action, flowing rhythms for reflection, fragments for shock.

Never repeat a striking phrase, image, or metaphor within a chapter. If you used an image once, find a different one. Each scene must advance; if dialogue or narration circles back to the same point, the chapter has stalled.

Keep metaphors grounded in a single domain. Don't mix sources within a passage — if you're comparing something to water, don't switch to fire mid-image. The best metaphors come from the character's own world.

**Rule:** After drafting, cut every word that doesn't earn its place. Read aloud — if you stumble, rewrite.

## 7. Scene Transitions

The space between scenes is where readers are lost or held. A transition must reorient in time and place, shift emotional register, and maintain momentum. The best transitions create a subtle connection between scenes — an echo of image, emotion, or theme that makes the cut feel intentional.

**Rule:** Never begin a new scene by re-establishing information the reader already has. Start at the moment something new happens.

## 8. Worldbuilding Through Immersion

Ground the fantasy world in concrete, specific details woven into action. Instead of explaining a magic system, show a character using it and paying its cost. Every fantastical element should have sensory texture — what does magic smell like? What does a ward feel like when you pass through it?

The world should have weight and consequence. History isn't backstory — it's the reason a character flinches at a particular sigil, or why one district has no trees. Culture isn't flavor text — it's why a character apologizes before drawing a weapon.

**Rule:** The reader should learn how the world works by watching characters navigate it, not by being told.

## 9. Magic and Wonder

The best fantasy makes the reader feel genuine wonder. This comes not from power levels or systematic rules but from cost, mystery, and beauty. Magic should be unsettling, awe-inspiring, or bittersweet — never routine.

When characters encounter the fantastical, skip the expected response. Fear can manifest as hysterical laughter or sudden hunger. Awe can feel like nausea. Wonder can arrive as irritation at the world refusing to make sense. Ground every reaction in specific physiological detail rather than named emotions.

**Rule:** Every use of magic should cost something — energy, sanity, memory, years of life, or moral compromise.

## 10. Pacing Across Length

A chapter of ~4000 words must breathe. Relentless action exhausts; unbroken reflection bores. Alternate between compression (fast, tense, external) and expansion (slow, reflective, internal). Short paragraphs and punchy sentences accelerate. Longer, rhythmic prose decelerates.

Each scene within a chapter should run 600-1200 words with its own mini-arc: a small goal, an obstacle, and a shift.

**Rule:** After every high-intensity beat, give the character a moment to absorb what happened — but make that quiet moment do double duty by advancing character or worldbuilding.

## 11. Originality

Avoid the first image that comes to mind — it's probably a cliche. Find the second or third. Never reach for generic metaphors (heart pounding, blood running cold, steeling oneself) when the character's own expertise offers something specific no other story would use.

Fantasy has deep conventions. Use them as a foundation, then subvert, complicate, or recontextualize. The reader should feel they're encountering something that couldn't exist in any other story.

**Rule:** For every scene, ask: "What would the reader expect here?" Then do something different — not for shock, but because reality is stranger and more interesting than convention.

## 12. Chapter Openings and Closings

Open in motion — arrive in the middle of something. Avoid "waking up" and "settling in" openings. Close on a pivot: a revelation, a decision, a question that won't let the reader stop. The last line of a chapter is the most important sentence you'll write.

**Rule:** Your opening paragraph should contain a concrete action, a sensory detail, and an unanswered question. Your closing paragraph should change what the reader thinks they know.
